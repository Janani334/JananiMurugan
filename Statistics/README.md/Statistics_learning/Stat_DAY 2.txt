STATISTICS DAY 2

1. OVERVIEW

Day 2 started with a revision of the Day 1 concepts and continued with
important statistical topics such as population, sample, sampling methods,
census, sampling bias, probability, skewness, quartiles, and correlation.

2. DAY 1 REVISION

The Day 1 concepts were revised, including types of statistics, types of
data, measurement scales, measures of variability, outliers, and descriptive
statistics.

Example:
Using mean, median, and standard deviation to understand a company's
sales dataset.

3. POPULATION

Population refers to the complete group of individuals or observations that
an analyst wants to study.

Example:
All customers of a company are considered the population when studying
customer satisfaction.

4. SAMPLE

A sample is a smaller group selected from a population for analysis.

Example:
Selecting 500 customers from all customers to conduct a satisfaction survey.

5. PARAMETER

A parameter is a numerical value that describes the entire population.

Example:
The average salary of all employees in a company is a population parameter.

6. STATISTIC

A statistic is a numerical value calculated from a sample.

Example:
The average salary calculated from 100 selected employees is a statistic.

7. SAMPLING

Sampling is the process of selecting a smaller group from a population for
analysis.

Example:
Instead of asking every customer for feedback, a company selects 1,000
customers and collects their opinions.

8. SAMPLING METHODS

Simple Random Sampling

Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected.

Example:
Randomly selecting 100 customer IDs from a customer database.

Systematic Sampling

Members are selected at a fixed interval from a list.

Example:
Selecting every 10th customer from a customer list.

Stratified Sampling

The population is divided into groups, and samples are selected from each
group.

Example:
Selecting employees from different departments such as HR, Sales, and IT.

9. CENSUS VS SAMPLING

Census

A census collects information from every member of the population.

Example:
A company asks every employee to complete a survey.

Sampling

Sampling collects information from only a selected portion of the population.

Example:
A company selects 200 employees to complete the survey.

Sampling is generally faster, less expensive, and easier to manage than a
census.

10. SAMPLING BIAS

Sampling bias occurs when the selected sample does not properly represent
the population.

Example:
A company wants to understand the opinions of all customers but surveys
only premium customers. The results may not represent regular customers.

11. PROBABILITY

Probability measures how likely an event is to occur.

Example:
When tossing a fair coin, the probability of getting Heads is 1/2 or 50%.

12. TYPES OF PROBABILITY

Joint Probability

Joint probability is the probability of two events occurring together.

Example:
Finding the probability that a customer is both a Premium Member and a
Product Buyer.

Marginal Probability

Marginal probability is the probability of a single event without
considering another event.

Example:
Finding the probability that a customer is a Premium Member.

13. SKEWNESS

Skewness describes the asymmetry or imbalance of a data distribution.

Symmetric Distribution

The data is distributed relatively equally on both sides.

Example:
A dataset where values are balanced around the center.

Positive Skewness

The distribution has a longer tail toward the right side.

Example:
Income data can be positively skewed because a small number of people
may have very high incomes.

Negative Skewness

The distribution has a longer tail toward the left side.

Example:
If most students score very high but a few students score very low,
the marks may be negatively skewed.

14. QUARTILES

Quartiles divide an ordered dataset into four parts.

Q1 – First Quartile
Approximately 25% of the data falls below Q1.

Q2 – Second Quartile
Approximately 50% of the data falls below Q2. Q2 is also the Median.

Q3 – Third Quartile
Approximately 75% of the data falls below Q3.

Example:
Quartiles can be used to divide employee salaries into lower, middle,
and higher salary groups.

15. CORRELATION

Correlation measures the strength and direction of the relationship between
two variables.

Positive Correlation

When one variable increases, the other variable tends to increase.

Example:
As study hours increase, exam marks may also increase.

Negative Correlation

When one variable increases, the other variable tends to decrease.

Example:
As product price increases, the number of units sold may decrease.

No Correlation

There is little or no relationship between two variables.

Example:
A person's shoe size and their exam marks generally have no meaningful
relationship.
